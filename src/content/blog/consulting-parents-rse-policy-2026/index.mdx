The consultation I see most often is a draft policy on the website with a comment box open for a fortnight. It satisfies nobody. The parents who were never going to read a policy don't read it, and the parent with a genuine question finds no one to ask, so the question arrives three months later as a complaint about a lesson that has already happened.

Since 1 September, this is a must rather than good practice. Schools "must proactively engage and consult parents when they develop and review their policy" ([paragraph 12 of the July 2025 RSHE guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5)). What the guidance doesn't do is tell you what a consultation looks like. There's no vote, no threshold, no quorum, and nothing that says how many parents have to reply. That's yours to design, which is good news, because the version that works is not the version that generates the most paperwork. If you want the wider picture of what changed in the guidance, I wrote a [plain English summary](/blog/2026-rse-guidance-plain-english-summary/).

## Work out what's actually on the table

Before you ask anybody anything, be honest internally about what could change as a result. Relationships education is compulsory. The statutory content is statutory. Safeguarding-driven decisions are not up for negotiation. Say so at the start, in the invitation itself.

That still leaves a lot that parents can genuinely shape: which term a topic lands in, how much notice families get before a sensitive lesson, which resources you use, who teaches it, the language you use with younger children, and what support the school offers parents having the same conversation at home. In primary, the content of any sex education you choose to teach beyond the science curriculum is open too.

Consultations go wrong when a parent spends four weeks arguing about something that was never movable and then discovers it was never movable. They feel ignored, and fairly so. Naming the boundary early is a kindness, not a defensive move.

## Show the teaching before you ask for views

Most of the anxiety I meet is about an imagined curriculum rather than the real one. A parent who has pictured something alarming is not going to be reassured by a policy document, because policy prose is exactly where an imagined curriculum can hide.

So lead with the lessons. Year group, topic, what it covers, what it doesn't. Ten minutes walking parents through the actual [puberty](/topics/puberty/) lesson their Year 5 child will get does more than any amount of consultation paperwork. The guidance points the same way: schools "should take steps to pro-actively engage parents and make sure they are aware of what is being taught in RSHE", inviting them in to discuss the curriculum and any concerns, and supporting them in managing the conversations at home ([paragraph 55](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=34)). Parents should also know about sex education content in advance of the lessons ([paragraph 15](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=6)).

## Open the materials on your own terms

Two separate obligations sit in one sentence of the guidance. Schools "should show parents a representative sample of the resources that they plan to use", and "should ensure that parents are able to view all curriculum materials used to teach RSHE on request" ([paragraph 56](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). A curriculum overview is not the sample, and the sample does not discharge the on-request access.

Do this before you're asked. A viewing session during the consultation, a parent portal login, the scheme open on a laptop in reception for a fortnight. Schools that get ahead of it rarely receive the formal, adversarial version of the request. If you've adapted a published scheme, show the version your classes will actually see and say which version it is.

The mechanics have some detail worth reading properly. Copyright still applies to how you share, with a portal or a presentation preferred over an open download, but it "should never be used as a reason to refuse to share" ([paragraphs 59 and 60](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). And any provider clause that stops you showing material to parents is void and unenforceable ([paragraphs 57 and 58](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). Reading your contracts for those clauses is a job with a lead time, and it sits on the wider [RSE policy checklist](/blog/rse-policy-checklist-2026/) alongside everything else the review needs.

## Reach the parents who won't come to the meeting

Your PTA is not your parent body. A survey catches volume; an evening session catches the people who want to be heard rather than counted; neither reaches the families who never respond to either. Those are usually the ones whose views you most need: parents with English as an additional language, parents of children with SEND, faith communities, and fathers, who are consistently under-represented in every consultation I've been part of.

Go to them. A conversation at the school gate, a translated summary, a question added to an existing meeting a family already attends. If forty responses came from the same forty families, say so in your write-up and do something about the gap next time. I've written separately about [the letters, questionnaires and classroom events](/blog/reduce-withdrawals-sex-education-parent-consultation/) that make this work in practice, including putting parents through an activity from the planned teaching.

## Ask questions that can be answered

"Do you support our RSE policy?" produces noise. Questions with somewhere to go produce something you can act on:

- At what age do you think children should learn about this, and what's behind that?
- What would you want to know before a lesson on this takes place?
- What would help you carry on the conversation at home?
- Is there anything about your family's faith or culture we should understand in how we teach this?

Leave room for uncertainty and for choosing not to answer. Offer a private route for anything about an individual child. And don't read silence as agreement.

## Write back

This is the part that's usually missing, and it's the part that decides whether the consultation was worth running. Publish a short response afterwards: the themes parents raised, what you changed because of them, and what you didn't change with the reason attached. "We can't move this, it's statutory" is a perfectly good reason. So is "we've kept this in Year 6 because of what we're seeing in school". Parents cope with disagreement far better than they cope with silence.

Be straight about the limit while you're there. "Parents are not able to veto curriculum content, but schools must consult with parents when developing their RSHE policy" ([paragraph 56](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). And don't let the right to withdraw do the work of consultation for you. "They can always withdraw" is not an answer to a concern about content, and it doesn't cover relationships education or science anyway. [How withdrawal actually works](/blog/parental-right-to-withdraw-rse-2026/) differs between primary and secondary, and a parent who has taken part in your consultation keeps the right regardless.

A word on organised responses. Twelve near-identical emails are twelve views. Record them, weigh them, and don't be stampeded by volume into a change the curriculum can't take. Equally, don't dismiss a concern that is genuinely widely held just because it arrived in a co-ordinated form.

## Primary has one extra

Primary schools "should consult parents about the content of anything that will be taught within sex education", along with supporting them to talk to their children about it and explaining the right to request withdrawal ([paragraph 31](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=12)). That's a consultation on the specific lessons, not a general one about the policy. If you're sorting out which of your lessons even count as sex education, that split is the first thing to get right in [what the guidance changes for primary](/blog/2026-rse-guidance-primary-schools/).

## Keep the record, and put the next one in the calendar

Dates, methods, how many parents you reached, the themes, what changed, and the governor minute approving the final policy. That record is what answers a complaint, a governor's question or an inspector a year later, when the person who made the decision has moved on.

The duty covers developing *and* reviewing the policy, so this isn't a one-off. Tie it to a fixed review date in the governance calendar and it will happen in the quiet years too, which are the years it's easiest to skip. If you're briefing a board on any of this, [the questions governors ask](/blog/rse-policy-questions-governors-ask/) covers the ones that tend to come up.

The thing consultation buys you isn't agreement. It's that when a disagreement does surface, it surfaces as a conversation with a parent you already know, rather than a letter from a stranger.
