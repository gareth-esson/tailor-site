## It's not just about Andrew Tate

Many of us are aware of and concerned about the influence of figures like
Andrew Tate on vulnerable boys and young men. The obvious response is a
lesson with his name on it, but naming him introduces him to the pupils
who had not heard of him, could send some of them searching, and gives him
disproportionate weight in the room.

He is not absent from the teaching. He appears where he is useful, in the
lesson on why this material is persuasive, because pretending pupils have
not heard of him fools nobody. What changes is that he is an example
rather than the subject.

## Separating the need from the answer

The reason this material reaches boys is that it attaches itself to things
that are not wrong to want. Confidence. Money. Fitness. A relationship. A
way through rejection. Somewhere to belong.

That distinction runs through every lesson in the sequence. It is also what
lets a boy who follows this content stay in the room, because the lesson is
not an accusation aimed at him.

## Not a unit about boys behaving badly

Restrictive ideas about masculinity harm boys too, including by making it
harder to ask for help. A sequence that spends five lessons telling boys
what not to be, and never offers a version of themselves worth having,
fails the pupils it most needs to reach. Girls in the room need the
analytical tools as much as anyone, not a supporting role in someone
else's lesson.

For Year 9 the sequence also stays away from incel terminology. The
government guidance on that material is written for Year 10 and above. The
ideas underneath it, rejection, entitlement, blame and dehumanisation, are
taught here without handing thirteen year olds the vocabulary.

## Start from what boys actually think

A staffroom usually arrives at this topic with a picture already formed, in
one direction or the other. Correcting it first makes the rest of the
planning easier, because a sequence built on the wrong premise about the
boys in the room teaches the wrong lesson however well it is delivered.

[Masculinity and misogyny: what do boys actually
think?](/blog/what-do-boys-actually-think/) is free and takes about five minutes.
Twelve survey findings, and you guess each number before you see it. The
gap between the guess and the answer is the useful part. Most people
underestimate how much pressure boys report, and badly overestimate how
much of the material they endorse. It runs as a staff meeting starter as
well as it reads alone.
