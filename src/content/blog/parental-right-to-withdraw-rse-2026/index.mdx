When a withdrawal request lands, the first job is working out what it can reach. Parents “have the right to request that their child be withdrawn from some or all of sex education delivered as part of statutory RSE” ([paragraph 16, printed page 6](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=7)). “Parents” in the guidance means parents and carers (footnote 3, printed page 2).

The DfE’s [July 2025 RSHE statutory guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf) [has applied in England since 1 September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education). Schools “must have regard to the guidance”, with good reasons needed to depart from its should or should-not statements (paragraph 5, printed page 3).

## What the right doesn't reach

“Parents do not have the right to withdraw their pupils from relationships and health education, nor can they be withdrawn from topics taught as part of the science curriculum, including science topics related to puberty or sexual reproduction” (paragraph 21, printed page 6). A request to take a child out of RSE altogether asks for more than the right allows.

The policy should “Differentiate between relationships and sex education (where sex education is taught), so that parents have clear information”, and relationships education “doesn't involve explaining the detail of different forms of sexual activity, but can cover sensitive topics such as sexual violence in order to keep children safe” (paragraph 14, printed page 4). Sexual violence taught within relationships education stays in place, and so does the science. Mark the strand each lesson belongs to before a request arrives.

## Primary and secondary answer differently

“Where primary schools provide sex education, head teachers must automatically grant a request to withdraw a pupil from it, other than content that is taught as part of the science curriculum. In secondary, head teachers can refuse a request in exceptional circumstances, for example because of safeguarding concerns or a pupil's specific vulnerability” (paragraph 18, printed page 6).

For secondary pupils, the guidance limits refusal to “exceptional circumstances”. The safeguarding concerns and specific vulnerability it names are examples, rather than a complete list. Keep the decision tied to the pupil’s circumstances.

## SEND doesn't change the category

Paragraph 18's example of “a pupil's specific vulnerability” concerns an individual secondary decision. It is not a blanket exception for SEND. Teaching “should be developed to ensure these subjects are accessible for pupils with SEND”, and schools should recognise that these pupils may be more vulnerable to abuse and exploitation (paragraphs 75 and 76, printed page 37). The primary automatic-grant rule quoted above still applies to sex education beyond the science curriculum.

## The conversation, and the record

Before granting a request, “it is good practice for the head teacher to discuss the request with parents, and the child if appropriate, to understand the request and to clarify the nature and purpose of the curriculum”. That is likely to cover the benefits, the detrimental effects including social and emotional impacts, and the chance of the child hearing “their peers' version of what was said in class, which may not be accurate”. Schools “will want to document this process to ensure a record is kept” (paragraph 17, printed page 6).

The guidance sets no form, notice period or deadline for this discussion. Any arrangements your policy adds should be identified as the school’s own process. Settle in advance who meets the parent and who confirms in writing which lessons the pupil will miss and what they will do instead.

## The pupil's decision, three terms before 16

“From three terms before the pupil turns 16, a pupil can choose to opt back into sex education even if their parent has requested withdrawal.” The guidance works its own example: “if a pupil turns 16 during the autumn term, the pupil can opt back into sex education at any time after the start of the previous autumn term.” It adds: “Schools should ensure that pupils know they have this option” (paragraph 19, printed page 6).

Plan how the school will explain that option to pupils, so it does not depend on them challenging their parents.

## The lessons they miss

“If a pupil is withdrawn from sex education, it is the school's responsibility to ensure that the pupil receives appropriate, purposeful education during the period of withdrawal” (paragraph 20, printed page 6). Purposeful means planned: name the work and the member of staff before the first lesson, not on the morning.

## Put it in the policy before anyone asks

The RSE policy should “Include information about a parent's right to request that their child is withdrawn from sex education” (paragraph 14, printed page 4), and should explain how teachers will handle questions that “relate to sex education from which they have been withdrawn”, “with an emphasis on supporting the child” (paragraph 87, printed page 40). Schools “should ensure parents are aware of sex education content within lessons in advance” (paragraph 15, printed page 5), and primaries consulting parents on sex education content should include “advice about parents' right to request withdrawal” (paragraph 31, printed page 11).
