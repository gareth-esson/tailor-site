The updated guidance [took effect this month](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education), so the 2019 document is now history and the question is no longer what's coming. For primary schools, most of the answer is that you carry on. The curriculum content is recognisably the same one you've been teaching. What's changed is a short list of additions, one clarification that will need a letter home before you teach it, and a much firmer set of expectations about what parents get to see.

## What hasn't changed

Relationships education is still compulsory across years 1 to 6. Sex education is still not compulsory in primary. Parents still have the right to request withdrawal from any sex education you do teach, and a primary head must still grant that request for anything outside the science curriculum ([paragraph 18](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=7)).

Much of families, caring friendships and being safe reads as it did in 2019. First aid, healthy eating and physical fitness are broadly untouched. If your scheme of work is solid, you're auditing it, not rebuilding it.

## The additions that need curriculum time

Four are straightforward to slot in:

- **Personal safety** is now a topic in its own right, covering hazards including fire, and risk around roads, railways and water, with the water safety code named.
- **Change and loss**, including bereavement, with the point that grief is a natural response and that everyone grieves differently.
- **Vaping**, alongside the existing drugs, alcohol and tobacco content, including nicotine addiction from products like pouches.
- **Online financial harms**, including gaming, video game monetisation, scams and fraud, plus why gambling sites and social media carry age restrictions. The minimum age for social media, currently 13, is now content pupils should know.


## The correct names for body parts are now explicit

Primary health education now lists them: penis, vulva, vagina, testicles, scrotum, nipples. Pupils should also understand that these parts of the body are private, and have the skills to express their own boundaries around them.

Plenty of primaries have taught this for years, because it's what lets a child describe what happened to them. The guidance is explicit that correct terms are a safeguarding matter and can be taught without describing any sexual activity ([paragraph 23](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=8)).

Plan for it anyway, because of what parents will assume when they see the words. This is health education, not sex education, and there is no right of withdrawal from it ([paragraph 21](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=7)). That's a far easier conversation to have in a letter in September than in response to a request in March. Our [guide to teaching puberty in primary schools](/blog/teaching-puberty-primary-schools-guide/) covers the classroom side.

## More skills, not just more knowledge

The shift that's easiest to miss in an audit is that a lot of the new material is something pupils should be able to do. Primary pupils are now expected to learn how to communicate effectively and manage conflict, how to be assertive and express needs and boundaries, and how to tell the difference between being assertive and being controlling. There's the same emphasis online, where they should learn strategies for resisting pressure to share information or images.

A curriculum map that checks topic coverage against the content lists will come back clean and still miss this. The question to ask of each lesson is whether pupils practise the skill in it or only hear about it.

## Sex education now has a year group attached

Sex education remains optional in primary, but the recommendation is no longer general: the DfE recommends it in years 5 and/or 6, in line with the conception and birth content that sits in the national curriculum for science ([paragraph 30](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=12)).

That makes the choice a more visible one. A school teaching no sex education is now departing from a specific recommendation rather than a vague one, and should be able to say why.

It also raises the definitional point that causes most of the confusion in primary. Your policy has to differentiate relationships education from sex education, so parents can tell what is and isn't withdrawable ([paragraph 14](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5)). Get that boundary written down precisely, because the science content and the health education content both sit outside the right to withdraw, and parents rarely know that until someone tells them.

## The late-primary discretion, and the condition attached to it

Schools may decide to discuss the pressure to share naked images in late primary where it's affecting their pupils, and may address online sexual content where they know pupils have seen pornography ([paragraph 29](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=8)). Teaching should be age appropriate and respectful of all children, including those with no familiarity with the topics at all.

Schools should inform parents in advance of any deviation from their published RSE policy and share the relevant materials. So if there's a realistic chance you'll need this in years 5 and 6, it belongs in the policy now. Writing it in this year is a routine consultation. Notifying it mid-year, after an incident, is a much harder letter to write.

## Openness with parents is the change with the sharpest edge

The expectation is no longer that materials are available on request. Schools should show parents a representative sample of the resources they plan to use ([paragraph 56](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)), with a parent portal named as best practice ([paragraph 59](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). Clauses in a provider's contract that stop you sharing any material at all with parents are void and unenforceable ([paragraph 58](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)), and copyright governs how you share materials, never whether you do ([paragraph 60](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)).

For most primaries this is the change that takes real work, because it means having the slides in a shareable state rather than in a shared drive. We've written separately about [how to run that consultation](/blog/consulting-parents-rse-policy-2026/).

## What isn't in it

The 2024 draft proposed hard age limits on when particular content could be taught. They [aren't in the final guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687648f6352c290d20dcae95/RSHE_consultation_response_-_15_July_2025.pdf#page=20). Sequencing is back with you, framed as careful sequencing and skilled delivery in the new guiding principles at [paragraph 15](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=6). That's a significant restoration of professional judgement, and it means the justification for your sequence now has to be yours.

The dedicated SEND section is also thinner than the 2019 one, with SEND expectations spread through the document instead. The practical effect is that nobody hands you a model for adaptation. Your adapted materials are your own evidence that the curriculum is accessible, so they're worth keeping where you can show them.

## Where to start this term

Audit against the content lists first, since that's quick and tells you the size of the job. Then do the two things that involve parents before you teach anything new: settle where sex education sits in years 5 and 6 and write the boundary into the policy, and decide whether the late-primary discretion applies to your school. Both are easier as a September conversation than a summer one.
