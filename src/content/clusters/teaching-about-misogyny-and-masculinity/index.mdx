## What this topic asks of a school

Most schools arrive at this topic from one of two directions. Either a
scheme of work needs writing, and misogyny has moved from an optional
unit to something the guidance expects to see covered. Or something has
already happened: a name circulating in Year 9, a comment in a corridor,
a group of boys repeating a line none of them would defend if asked to.

The two starting points need different first moves. A planned unit can
build from stereotypes, through influence, to the specific content pupils
are seeing. A response to an incident has to begin where the pupils
already are, which is usually further along than the planning assumes.

What both need is a teacher who is not going into the room to win.

## What a sequence usually has to cover

Schools vary, and this is not a scheme of work. It is the shape most
sequences end up taking once the obvious gaps are filled in.

**Stereotypes before influencers.** Pupils who have never named a gender
stereotype out loud have nothing to measure an influencer against. Year
7 and 8 work on expectations, who sets them and who pays for them, does
more for the later lessons than the later lessons do on their own.

**The double standard, in pupils' own words.** The clearest way into
misogyny is usually something pupils have already noticed: the same
behaviour named differently depending on who does it. It is a question
they bring unprompted, which makes it a better opening than an
influencer's name.

**Influence as a mechanism, not a person.** A lesson built around one
figure ages badly and hands that figure the attention. A lesson about how
monetised advice reaches a fourteen year old, who profits, and what the
business model needs them to believe, survives the next name.

**Where it meets consent and image sharing.** Misogyny is rarely a unit
on its own by Key Stage 4. It runs into consent, into what gets forwarded
in a group chat, and into what pupils have already seen. Plan the
overlap deliberately rather than teaching it three times.

**Somewhere for boys to stand.** A sequence that only tells boys what not
to be leaves them with nothing, and they notice. The lessons that land
give them a version of themselves worth having.

## What tends to go wrong

The failure is usually not the content. It is the room.

Debating an influencer on his own terms makes him the subject of the
lesson and the teacher his opponent, in front of an audience. Shaming the
boy who repeated the line teaches everyone else to say it somewhere else.
And treating the whole class as suspects loses the pupils who were
already on your side.
