# Tailor Education — Blog Writing Rules

For use by Claude Code when drafting blog posts for tailoreducation.org.uk. These rules describe how Gareth Esson writes and what the Tailor blog voice sounds like. Follow them to get a draft to 90% — Gareth will do the final 10%.

*For showcase, service and landing page copy — which uses the organisation's "we" voice rather than Gareth's first-person blog voice — see the sibling doc `Tailor_Site_Copy_Writing_Rules.md`.*

---

**Golden rule: it should read like advice from an experienced colleague, not an essay.**

- **Author persona:** Gareth Esson, RSE practitioner — not an academic, policy commentator or journalist. Uses "I" and "we" naturally, reflects, is honest about complexity, can be opinionated but from experience with young people rather than frustration with the system.
- **Never write experience Gareth hasn't had.** No invented anecdotes, no "in my experience", "the schools I visit", "most of the anxiety I meet" unless it's something he has actually said. A first-person observation is his to make; it can't be manufactured on his behalf, and it can't be quietly upgraded into a claim about the sector. Where a first-person observation would go, write the point without one and leave the gap — first-person material is part of Gareth's 10%.
- **Reader:** teachers, PSHE leads, DSLs, school leaders, sometimes parents. They do not need to be convinced to read on; they will if the content is useful. Write at practitioner level — don't explain what they already know.

## Structure

- No fixed word count — as long as the job needs.
- **Opening:** a straightforward introduction to the piece. No clickbait. The reader clicked the link. They already want the advice. No hook, no problem statement, no "here's why this matters" before the thing that matters.
- **Body:** prose with short plain headings. Not bullet lists unless absolutely necessary.
- **Closing:** one or two lines, natural CTA, no heading, no sales language. A flat ending is fine — don't add a rhetorical payoff to a reader who came for a method.
- **Trusted resources:** a section at the end with links, when the post cites guidance, tools or organisations.

## Tone

- Varied sentence lengths.
- Direct without being blunt; don't perform expertise; don't condescend.
- Serious topics get weight, never dramatic effect. Never open on someone else's trauma.
- **Assume good faith in schools and with parents.** A statement about the whole sector is fine when it describes something acting on schools from the outside — funding, workload, a guidance change landing mid-year. It isn't fine when it attributes the shortcoming to schools themselves. "Most schools are chronically under-funded" doesn't implicate the reader. "The consultation most schools run is a comment box" does. Where the gap is something schools are doing, describe the better version rather than the common one.
- **Don't characterise the absent party as failing.** Parents who don't reply to a survey, schools that haven't updated a scheme, families who don't come to events: describe what happened, not what it says about them. "The parents who were never going to read a policy" writes off the people the post exists to serve.

## Things to avoid (the AI-tells list)

- Three-item em-dash lists
- "It's not X; it's Y"
- Bold labels inside sections
- **Throat clearing anywhere, not just at the top.** No mini-intros, run-ups, or framing sentences that preview or characterise a point before making it. "Primary has an additional duty, and it's a different one rather than a larger one" → state the duty. "Two obligations sit in one sentence" → give the two obligations. "The guidance points the same way" → give the citation. Just say the thing.
- Filler intensifiers (dramatically, significantly, crucially, incredibly)
- Summary paragraphs
- Rhetorical questions as transitions
- Balanced triplets

## Practical

- British spelling.
- Hyperlink sources in text, no footnotes.
- Every post links to at least one Tailor `/topics/` landing page.
- Real, verifiable examples only — no invented composites.
- Cite DfE/Ofsted/statutory guidance specifically (name + year), but the guidance supports the argument, it isn't the argument.
- Check claims about what the guidance says against the source text, including what the previous version said before describing something as new.

## Two tests

- **The 90% test:** a complete publishable draft, no `[PLACEHOLDER]` tags — make real decisions.
- **The read-aloud test:** does it sound like one person start to finish, or does a more formal writer take over after the opening?
