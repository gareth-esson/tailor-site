I use letters and questionnaires to involve parents in sex education. We've also held coffee mornings and events where we put parents in the classroom. Parents get to experience some of the teaching themselves and ask about what they have seen.

Reducing withdrawals starts with helping parents feel confident about their child taking part. A parent may have an inaccurate idea of the lessons, or a concern that needs addressing in the teaching. Consultation gives you a chance to find that out and respond before lessons begin. It also helps parents connect the learning with what they want for their child.

## Start the conversation early

Begin while there is still time to respond. A letter home can explain the proposed teaching and why it belongs in the programme. Include a way to see the materials, send questions and arrange a private conversation. Give parents enough information to comment meaningfully.

In England, schools "must proactively engage and consult parents" when developing and reviewing their RSE policy ([July 2025 RSHE statutory guidance, paragraph 12](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5)). The guidance [has applied since September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education); the [full consultation guide](/blog/consulting-parents-rse-policy-2026/) explains the policy and materials-sharing detail.

## What the questionnaire needs to reveal

My questionnaires contain a range of questions. For a school putting one together, organising it into sections can help distinguish different concerns. One section can explore how familiar parents are with the RSE programme and what needs explaining. Another can ask what they want their child to learn about relationships and growing up. Give separate space to worries about their child's experiences outside school and views on the proposed lessons and resources. Include questions about support parents would welcome at home and how they would like to be involved.

Use the differences in those answers. Uncertainty about the content calls for a clearer explanation; a concern about a particular resource needs a response about that resource. Parents' knowledge of how their child communicates can help you adapt the teaching.

Combine response scales with space for parents to explain. Allow uncertainty, no concern and a choice not to answer. Offer a private route for individual matters, and do not count silence as support for the programme.

## In the classroom with parents

A coffee morning gives parents a chance to raise a tentative question and follow the answer up straight away. Have the people who will teach the lessons there, with materials available. Parents can get to know their approach and ask about anything that worries them.

In a classroom session, let parents try an activity from the planned teaching. Explain its purpose and where it sits in the programme, then use the actual materials. Parents can ask about the language used and how the activity would be adapted for their child's age or needs.

This makes the facilitation visible, too. Explain your ground rules and how you handle an unexpected question. Parents can see how you would support a pupil who feels uncomfortable. Leave time to hear what they make of the approach and whether they think it suits their child.

Offer other ways to take part for parents who cannot attend. Sharing materials and arranging another conversation helps keep the discussion open beyond the people in the room.

## From concerns to a reason to take part

Individual questions within that wider questionnaire can help identify shared priorities. In a special school, for example, I ask questions like: "To what extent are you concerned about your child's emerging sexuality?"

The answer needs exploring. Emerging sexuality is ordinary development; a parent's concern might relate to privacy, growing independence or how their child can communicate a boundary. It is also worth asking what they want their child to be able to understand and do. Show how a lesson would help their child practise one of those skills, using language or communication aids they can use. That connects the teaching to something the parent wants their child to be able to do.

Questions about social media's effect on wellbeing, or a child encountering sexually explicit material, can open up similar discussions. Explain which lessons address the concern, what pupils will do and why you have chosen that approach. The [secondary RSE guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=17), for instance, covers, under online safety, misleading portrayals of sexual behaviour in pornography and their possible effects on how people treat sexual partners. Show parents how the planned lessons approach that subject.

Explain the [different curriculum strands](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5) accurately. A parent's concern about social media does not establish agreement to sex education. Work through the actual lessons with them.

## After the consultation

The follow-up matters. Write back about the themes parents raised, answer outstanding questions and explain any changes you have made. That might involve adapting a resource or making the sequence of learning clearer. Keep individual children's circumstances out of the general response. Explain any limits: "Parents are not able to veto curriculum content" ([paragraph 56 of the guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)).

If a parent still wants to withdraw their child, listen to their particular reasons and explain the relevant process. The right to request withdrawal from "some or all of sex education" remains ([July 2025 RSHE statutory guidance, paragraph 16](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=7)); the [full withdrawal guide](/blog/parental-right-to-withdraw-rse-2026/) explains the phase differences and the pupil's own choice. Completing a questionnaire or attending an event does not waive that right.
