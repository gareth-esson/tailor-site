For a lot of schools the drop day is the RSE programme. One day, a specialist in the building, every year group covered, and the rest of the timetable left where it is. It is a real delivery model, and a well-planned day does things a weekly slot struggles to: one consistent voice, a whole cohort hearing the same content in the same week, and someone in the room who will answer the hard question without flinching.


What a single day cannot do on its own is show progression, and progression is what the statutory guidance asks for. The work, then, sits around the day rather than instead of it.


## What inspection actually looks at


There are no deep dives. The phrase appears nowhere in Ofsted's [education inspection framework, for inspections from 10 November 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/education-inspection-framework-eif/education-inspection-framework-for-use-from-november-2025), and nowhere in the [state-funded school inspection toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/school-inspection-toolkit-operating-guide-and-information) that replaced the school inspection handbook in November 2025. There is no overall effectiveness grade either: "From November 2025, we no longer provide an overall effectiveness grade for any provider inspected under the framework."


Schools now get a grade for each evaluation area: safeguarding, inclusion, curriculum and teaching, achievement, attendance and behaviour, personal development and wellbeing, leadership and governance, plus early years and post-16 provision where they apply. The scale is five points, running from urgent improvement to exceptional, and safeguarding is graded met or not met. Quality of education, personal development, and behaviour and attitudes are not evaluation area names.


RSE sits inside personal development and wellbeing. The [toolkit, June 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a915d1c01bbff0bf8f97b51/Schools_inspection_toolkit.pdf#page=50) puts it in the expected standard for personal development and wellbeing: "The personal development programme includes a suitable and well taught RHE/RSHE programme, which develops pupils' knowledge." The strong standard asks for more than coverage — "Pupils develop secure and detailed knowledge across the personal, social, health and economic education, relationships and (where relevant) sex education programme and citizenship curriculum" — and the [urgent improvement](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a915d1c01bbff0bf8f97b51/Schools_inspection_toolkit.pdf#page=52) list includes a curriculum that "fails to teach pupils about healthy relationships in line with the statutory relationships and sex education guidance".


Read those three together and the toolkit says nothing about the shape of your delivery. What it grades is whether the programme is well taught and whether pupils end up knowing the material. A drop day can meet that. So can a weekly lesson, and either can fail it.


Ofsted's [review of sexual abuse in schools and colleges, June 2021](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/review-of-sexual-abuse-in-schools-and-colleges/review-of-sexual-abuse-in-schools-and-colleges) set out what that looks like when it goes wrong. Its visits "identified a number of issues that meant that children and young people were not getting the quality of education in this subject that they should be. These included weak implementation of RSHE, poor teacher subject knowledge, and significant gaps in curriculum coverage." Children and young people themselves "were seldom positive about their RSHE and PSHE lessons".


## What one day can carry, and what it can't


RSE covers some of the most complex, personal and emotionally charged material in the curriculum. Consent. Pornography. Abuse. Identity. Young people need time to process it, to come back with questions, and to meet the same concepts again in different contexts as they get older.


The guidance expects that sequencing. Secondary RSE should "provide a clear progression from primary relationships education" ([paragraph 32, printed page 11](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=12)), and the health education content carries the same expectation, that teaching in secondary "should build on primary content" (paragraph 41, printed page 25). A day planned in isolation, to the same shape each year, cannot show that. A day planned against what the year group covered last year can.


A spiral curriculum, where key themes come back at increasing depth across year groups, is one way to lay that out, and the Sex Education Forum's Curriculum Design Tool sets out a version of it you can work from. Puberty education in Year 5 builds on body awareness in Year 3. [Online safety](/topics/online-safety) in Year 10 builds on the consent and boundary-setting work that came before it.


## Making the day carry weight


Bring your curriculum map to the planning call, not a topic list. The person delivering needs to know what this cohort covered last year and what they will cover next, because that is what turns six sessions into the next step in a sequence rather than a repeat of one.


Sequence the day by year group so the content escalates. If Year 8 and Year 10 get the same consent session, the day has told Year 10 nothing new.


Put one lesson either side of it. A tutor session beforehand that puts the vocabulary in place, and a follow-up session afterwards that answers the questions the day raised. A question box on the day gives you the agenda for that follow-up, and it is also the clearest evidence you will get of what landed.


Record who was there. The toolkit's [expected standard](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a915d1c01bbff0bf8f97b51/Schools_inspection_toolkit.pdf#page=51) says the personal development programme "is an entitlement for every pupil" and that "Leaders track participation". A drop day makes that easy to check and easy to fail: the pupils who were off timetable, on a trip or absent that day have missed the year's RSE, and someone needs a plan for them.


Write the day into the scheme of work as a unit, with its content listed. A day that exists only in the calendar cannot be quoted back to a governor, a parent or an inspector.


If you are setting a day up from scratch, the [logistics of getting one right](/blog/drop-day-setup/) matter as much as the content. Timing, room and what the students have been told before they walk in will decide how the sessions go.


## Who teaches it


Asked which actions would most improve RSE, young people put one above everything else: "Provide training for teachers so they can develop more confidence with RSE", selected by 57% of respondents ([Sex Education Forum, Young People's RSE Poll 2024, page 25](https://www.sexeducationforum.org.uk/sites/default/files/field/attachment/Young%20Peoples%20RSE%20Poll%202024%20-%20Report.pdf#page=25)). Young people can tell when a teacher is uncomfortable with the material, and it undermines the session.


That is an argument for bringing in a specialist, and it is also an argument for the lessons either side. The follow-up works when the tutor taking it has had the same training as the visitor, or close to it. [Training](/services/rse-training) that shifts delivery is sustained rather than a briefing at the start of term, and long enough to build both subject knowledge and the facilitation skills these topics ask for.


Specialist input and a staffed programme are not alternatives. I work with schools to deliver specific sessions and to co-teach with their staff, and the days that work best are the ones where the school's own teachers are in the room and pick the thread up in the weeks that follow.


## Trusted resources

- [Education inspection framework, for use from November 2025, Ofsted](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/education-inspection-framework-eif/education-inspection-framework-for-use-from-november-2025). The evaluation areas, the five-point scale, and the end of overall effectiveness grades.
- [School inspection toolkit, operating guide and information, Ofsted](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/school-inspection-toolkit-operating-guide-and-information). The toolkit is the graded standards; RSHE sits under personal development and wellbeing at pages 44 to 52.
- [Review of sexual abuse in schools and colleges, Ofsted, June 2021](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/review-of-sexual-abuse-in-schools-and-colleges/review-of-sexual-abuse-in-schools-and-colleges). The RSHE findings sit under "How are schools successfully delivering the new RSHE curriculum and how can they be supported further?".
- [Young People's RSE Poll 2024, Sex Education Forum, April 2024](https://www.sexeducationforum.org.uk/resources/evidence/young-peoples-rse-poll-2024). Full tables, including what young people asked government for, at page 25 of the report.
- [Curriculum Design Tool, Sex Education Forum](https://www.sexeducationforum.org.uk/resources/advice-guidance/curriculum-design-tool). A spiral, developmental approach to structuring an RSE programme from early years through secondary.
- [Programme of Study for PSHE education (key stages 1 to 5), PSHE Association, 2026](https://pshe-association.org.uk/guidance/ks1-5/programme-of-study). Free to download, and maps suggested learning opportunities against the statutory RSHE content.
