Most of the RSE I see taught in schools is not taught by RSE specialists. It's taught by whoever had the free period, or the form tutor, or the science teacher who drew the short straw. And more often than not the topic they have been handed is one of the hard ones: pornography, misogyny, consent, sexual violence. Little notice, no specialism, and a room of teenagers who can tell in seconds when an adult is uncomfortable.

If that's you this week, this is written for you.


## You don't need to be an expert. You need to be steady.


The fear with these topics is usually that a student will ask something you can't answer, or say something that derails the whole lesson. Here is the reassuring part: your job is not to know everything about pornography or masculinity. Your job is to be the calm adult who doesn't panic when the subject comes up. Students are not looking for a lecture. They are checking whether this is a room where they can ask a real question and get an honest, unembarrassed answer. You can offer that without being a specialist.


## What actually happens in the room


Less than you fear. When I teach [porn literacy](/blog/teaching-porn-literacy-what-it-is-why-it-matters/) to a Year 10 class, the lesson is not graphic and it is not a free-for-all. It's a conversation about why the thing they have almost certainly already seen is not a reliable guide to real sex, real bodies, or real consent. Most of the room is relieved that an adult is finally saying it out loud. The same is true of [conversations about masculinity and the influencers shaping it](/blog/talking-to-boys-masculinity-misogyny-online-influencers/): the boys are rarely hostile, they are curious, and they have usually never been asked what they actually think.


## The questions that come up, and what to do with them


Students will test you. "Have you watched porn, sir?" "Isn't Andrew Tate just telling the truth?" The instinct is to shut these down. Don't. A question like that is a student checking whether you'll be honest or performative. You don't have to answer a personal question, and you can say so plainly: "I'm not going to talk about myself, but I'll answer the real question underneath it." Then answer the real one. Most provocations are a genuine question wearing a costume.


Keep one move in your pocket for anything you can't answer: "That's a good question. I don't want to guess at it, so I'll find out and we'll come back to it." That sentence buys you time and models the exact behaviour you want students to have.


## How to not flinch


Flinching is the thing students read fastest. It tells them the topic is shameful and the adult can't cope, which is the opposite of what RSE is for. You reduce flinching by preparing the two or three moments you're dreading in advance. Decide now what you'll say if a student discloses something, if a question is deliberately explicit, or if the room goes silent. You won't script the whole lesson, but scripting the hard moments means you meet them with a plan instead of your face.


## The statutory grounding is on your side


It helps to remember that none of this is you going off-piste. [The 2026 statutory guidance](/blog/2026-rse-guidance-plain-english-summary/) explicitly requires secondary schools to teach about pornography, online misogyny, sexual ethics, and [consent](/blog/teaching-consent-beyond-no-means-no/). You are not introducing controversial material of your own. You are teaching the curriculum the DfE has told your school to teach. If a parent or a colleague questions it, that grounding is most of the answer.


## If it's still too much to carry alone


Some teachers read all of this and still, reasonably, don't want to teach FGM or sexual violence cold to a class they see every day. That's a fair position, not a failure. Sometimes the right call is to have a specialist teach the hardest sessions [directly to your students](/services/rse-for-secondary-schools), so the material is handled well and your ongoing relationship with the class isn't the thing carrying it. Sometimes it's [training for the whole team](/services/rse-training) so no one is improvising alone. Either way, being handed a hard topic with no support is a staffing problem, not a personal shortcoming. Name it as one.


The teachers who do this well are almost never the ones who felt ready. They are the ones who decided that a slightly awkward, honest lesson beats a polished silence. Students take the honesty every time.
