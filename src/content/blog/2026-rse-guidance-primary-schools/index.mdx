Sort this year's teaching into three piles before you do anything else with the revised guidance: relationships education, health education, and anything that counts as sex education. The three are treated differently, and the split runs through your policy, your parent communication and the right to withdraw. A curriculum map with one column headed RSHE won't tell you which pile a lesson belongs in.

The DfE's revised [RSHE statutory guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf), published in July 2025, [came into effect in England on 1 September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education). Schools “must have regard to the guidance”, and need good reasons to depart from the parts saying they should or should not do something (paragraph 5, printed page 3). Paragraph 2 says the regulations “make relationships education compulsory for all pupils receiving primary education” and “make health education compulsory in all schools except independent schools”. PSHE remains compulsory in independent schools; the health exception does not cover academies or free schools (printed pages 2 to 3, footnote 4). Primary sex education is treated differently.

## Years 5 and 6 is a recommendation, not a boundary

Sex education “is not compulsory in primary schools, but we recommend that primaries teach sex education in years 5 and/or 6, in line with content about conception and birth, which forms part of the national curriculum for science” ([paragraph 30, printed page 11](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=12)). The [2019 guidance](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/33650/1/Relationships_Education__Relationships_and_Sex_Education__RSE__and_Health_Education.pdf) recommended a programme “tailored to the age and the physical and emotional maturity of the pupils”, without naming a year group (2019 paragraph 67, page 23).

The 2024 consultation asked about “the restriction on teaching sex education only in years 5 or 6”, and the Department then “decided not to introduce age limits on certain topics in the RSHE statutory guidance but is clear on the need for content to be age appropriate” ([consultation response, July 2025, pages 14 and 20](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687648f6352c290d20dcae95/RSHE_consultation_response_-_15_July_2025.pdf#page=20)). What survived is a recommendation about where sex education usually sits, not a rule against teaching sooner when pupils need it, provided the content is age appropriate and parents have been consulted (paragraph 31).

## The relationships content asks for more practice

Read the content tables for what pupils have to do, not only what they have to know. Caring friendships now includes that “most people feel lonely sometimes, and that there is no shame in feeling lonely or talking about it” (printed page 8). The 2019 table treated loneliness as something healthy friendships avoid causing (2019, page 21).

Respectful, kind relationships carries the bigger shift. Pupils should have opportunities to discuss “the difference between being assertive and being controlling”, set healthy boundaries, and know how to challenge a stereotype rather than only recognise one (printed page 9). In 2019 that table asked for permission-seeking and what a stereotype is (2019, pages 21 to 22). Find the lesson where a child rehearses saying no to a friend. If there isn't one, that's your first gap.

## Late primary, online content and paragraph 29

Paragraph 29 says protective and preventative content should reach primary children “in a way that does not cause unreasonable alarm and does not appear to normalise risky behaviours or activities”. It then says that “in late primary, schools may decide to discuss the pressure to share naked images if this is affecting pupils in the school”, and may feel the need to discuss online sexual content where they know pupils have seen pornography (printed pages 7 to 8).

That is permission to respond, not an instruction to cover it with every Year 6. The same paragraph says teaching “should be age appropriate and respectful of all children”, and that schools should inform parents in advance of any deviation from their published RSE policy and share relevant materials on request. The online safety table has widened too, taking in privacy settings, the minimum age for joining social media sites, and that once a picture has been circulated “there is no way of deleting it everywhere” (printed page 10).

## Puberty planning may need to move

Developing bodies now names the terms pupils should know, including penis, vulva, vagina, testicles and scrotum, and asks that pupils understand those parts are private (printed page 25). On menstruation it is direct: “whilst the average age of the onset of menstruation is twelve, periods can start at eight, so covering this topic before girls' periods start will help them understand what to expect and avoid distress.” The 2019 wording asked for key facts about puberty “particularly from age 9 through to age 11” (2019, page 35).

If your first period lesson lands in the summer term of Year 5, that sentence is an argument for moving it. Paragraph 39 also asks for language such as period pads rather than sanitary items (printed page 20). Our [guide to teaching puberty in primary schools](/blog/teaching-puberty-primary-schools-guide/) covers the classroom side.

## Withdrawal, briefly

Where primary schools provide sex education, “head teachers must automatically grant a request to withdraw a pupil from it, other than content that is taught as part of the science curriculum” (paragraph 18, printed page 6). There is no right of withdrawal from relationships education, health education, or science topics related to puberty or sexual reproduction (paragraph 21). Primaries should also consult parents on the content of any sex education (paragraph 31).
