Most of the RSE I see taught in schools is not taught by RSE specialists. It's taught by whoever had the free period, or the form tutor, or the science teacher who drew the short straw. And more often than not the topic they have been handed is one of the hard ones: [pornography](/topics/pornography-and-media-literacy), misogyny, consent, sexual violence. Little notice, no specialism, and a room of teenagers who can tell in seconds when an adult is uncomfortable.

If that's you this week, this is written for you.


## You don't need to be an expert. You need to be steady.


The fear is usually a student asking something you can't answer, or derailing the lesson with a comment. Your job is not to know everything about pornography or masculinity. It's to be the calm adult who doesn't panic when the subject comes up. Students aren't looking for a lecture. They're checking whether this is a room where they can ask a real question and get an honest answer. Being calm is part of the job. Preparation matters too: read the lesson and its teacher guidance, and ask your subject lead about anything you are unsure how to teach.


## What actually happens in the room


Less than you fear. When I teach [porn literacy](/blog/teaching-porn-literacy-what-it-is-why-it-matters/) to a Year 10 class, the lesson is neither graphic nor a free-for-all. It's a conversation about why pornography is a poor guide to real sex, real bodies and real consent. Some of that class will have watched plenty and some will have seen none, and you can't tell which by looking. Pitch it for both.


[Conversations about masculinity and the influencers shaping it](/blog/talking-to-boys-masculinity-misogyny-online-influencers/) go much the same way. In my sessions the boys are rarely hostile. They're curious, and most have never been asked what they think. Some rooms are harder, which is a reason to plan.


## The questions that come up, and what to do with them


Students will test you. "Have you watched porn, sir?" "Isn't Andrew Tate just telling the truth?" The instinct is to shut these down; you don't have to. You also needn't answer a personal question, and can say so plainly: "I'm not going to talk about myself, but I'll answer the real question underneath it." Then answer that one, if it belongs in the lesson you planned.


Not every provocation is a sincere question in disguise. Plenty are. Some are a bid for status in front of mates, where a flat, unbothered answer beats a telling-off. Some may raise a concern about a pupil. Acknowledge it without inviting details in front of the class, explain that you will help, and follow the school's safeguarding procedure promptly. Your school's policy should say how teachers handle questions beyond what the school teaches, and the guidance recognises that staff may need training to support pupils with those ([paragraph 87](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=41)).


Keep one move in your pocket for anything you can't answer: "That's a good question. I don't want to guess at it, so I'll find out and we'll come back to it." It buys time and models the behaviour you want.


If a comment would harm someone else in the room, take on the idea, not the child. Anything sounding like a disclosure goes to your designated safeguarding lead, not into open discussion, and never promise a pupil you won't tell anyone ([paragraph 85](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=40)).


## How to not flinch


Flinching is the thing students read fastest. It tells them the topic is shameful and the adult can't cope, which is the opposite of what RSE is for. You reduce it by preparing the moments you're dreading: what you'll say if a student discloses something, if a question is deliberately explicit, or if the room goes silent. Script those and you meet them with a plan instead of your face.


## The statutory grounding is on your side


None of this is you going off-piste. Schools "must have regard to" the [DfE's statutory RSHE guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education), published July 2025 and applying from 1 September 2026, and need good reasons to depart from what it says they should do ([paragraph 5](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=4)). By the end of secondary, schools should have covered pornography's influence on sexual attitudes and behaviours, the role of [consent](/blog/teaching-consent-beyond-no-means-no/), recognising misogyny, and the possible influence of particular sub-cultures on sexual ethics ([guidance page 14](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=15)). Our [step-by-step RSE review](/blog/2026-rse-guidance-plain-english-summary/) covers checking your own programme against it.


For parents, connect that curriculum explanation to the actual teaching you have planned. Schools should show parents a representative sample of planned resources and let them see materials on request ([paragraph 56](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). Show how you will use the materials, make room for questions and respond to what parents raise. It isn't permission to improvise either: what you teach still has to be planned, age appropriate and checked first.


## If it's still too much to carry alone


Some teachers read this and still don't want to teach FGM or sexual violence cold to a class they see daily. That's a fair position, not a failure. Sometimes the right call is a specialist teaching the hardest sessions [directly to your students](/services/rse-for-secondary-schools), so your relationship with the class isn't carrying it. Sometimes it's [training for the whole team](/services/rse-training) so nobody improvises alone. Being handed a hard topic with no support is a staffing problem, not a personal shortcoming. Name it as one.


You can teach this while still feeling nervous. Know what you are teaching, prepare the questions you can anticipate, and know who will support you with the ones you cannot.
