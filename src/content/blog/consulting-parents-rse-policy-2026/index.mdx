Consulting on your policy, explaining the teaching and sharing the materials are separate jobs. Parents need a clear way to take part in each.

For England, the [July 2025 RSHE statutory guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf) [took effect on 1 September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education). Schools “must have regard to the guidance” and need good reasons for departing from its should or should-not statements (paragraph 5, printed page 3).

## The part that is a must

“All schools must have an up-to-date written policy for relationships education or, where they teach sex education, for RSE. Schools must proactively engage and consult parents when they develop and review their policy” ([paragraph 12, printed page 4](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5)). The same paragraph says schools “must provide a copy of their policy free of charge to anyone who asks for one and publish the policy on the school website”. Its footnote adds: “If a school does not have a website, they should ensure that the policy is available by other means” (paragraph 12 and footnote 7, printed page 4).

Health education is different: schools “are not required to publish a policy for health education”, though “it would be good practice for schools to set out some of the details about how health education will be taught” (paragraph 13, printed page 4). Primary schools have one addition — they “should consult parents about the content of anything that will be taught within sex education”, including support for parents in talking to their children and advice about the right to request withdrawal (paragraph 31, printed page 11).

## Consultation isn't a referendum

“Parents are not able to veto curriculum content, but schools must consult with parents when developing their RSHE policy … and schools should respond positively to requests from parents to see material” (paragraph 56, printed page 34).

Explain that boundary when you invite parents to contribute. Consultation cannot remove compulsory content. The guidance prescribes no vote, threshold or quorum; avoid presenting a local process as a statutory requirement. What consultation can shape is sequencing, framing, the support offered to parents, and in primary the content of the sex education you choose to teach.

## Explaining it, and hearing the questions

Schools “should take steps to pro-actively engage parents and make sure they are aware of what is being taught in RSHE”, by inviting them in to discuss the curriculum and any concerns, and “supporting parents in managing conversations with their children about RSHE topics” (paragraph 55, printed page 33). Schools should also make sure parents are aware of sex education content in lessons in advance (paragraph 15, printed page 5).

## Sharing your resources and handling provider contracts

“Schools should show parents a representative sample of the resources that they plan to use, enabling parents to continue conversations started in class, and should ensure that parents are able to view all curriculum materials used to teach RSHE on request” (paragraph 56, printed page 34). Both halves matter: a summary of your curriculum isn't the sample, and the sample isn't access to everything on request. If you have adapted a published scheme, show the version your classes will see, and say which version it is.

External providers need settling at the contract stage: schools “should not agree to any contractual restrictions on showing parents any content that the school will use”, and should tell providers that all content can be shared with parents (paragraph 57, printed page 34). Clauses that “seek to prevent schools sharing any material at all with parents” are “void and unenforceable” (paragraph 58, printed page 34). That concerns clauses blocking sharing with parents; it isn't a general right to reuse or republish someone else's material.

## Copyright, and how the sharing happens

“Where copyright law applies, schools must comply with it when sharing resources with parents.” Best practice is sharing via a parent portal, “or, if this is not possible, through a presentation”. Either way the school should acknowledge the provider's authorship and “include a statement, that parents agree to as a condition of access, that the content should not be copied or shared further except as authorised under copyright law”. Where relevant and possible, IT systems “should also be in place to prevent downloading” (paragraph 59, printed page 34). The guidance names no particular product. If you use an existing school system, check that it can meet these sharing conditions.

Where parents can't use the portal or attend, schools “can provide copies of materials to parents to take home, providing parents agree to a similar statement that they will not copy the content or share it further except as authorised under copyright law”. The guidance is clear: “copyright law affects the way in which materials are shared, but should never be used as a reason to refuse to share them” (paragraph 60, printed page 34).

## Keeping the policy true after the meeting

Plans change during the year. Schools “may need to include new content in RSHE to respond to emerging needs or issues in the school but should be careful to inform parents of any shifts away from the policy and continue to share relevant materials on request” (paragraph 12, printed page 4). For primary schools’ late-primary protective content, the guidance says to inform parents of policy deviations “in advance” (paragraph 29, printed pages 7 to 8).

The policy itself should “Set out how parents can view curriculum materials” (paragraph 14, printed page 5). I'd keep a dated note of the questions, responses and changes for the next review. That record is a practical suggestion.
