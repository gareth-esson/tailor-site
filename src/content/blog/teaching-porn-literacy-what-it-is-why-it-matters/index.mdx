Most teachers handed pornography to teach are clear about why it belongs on the curriculum and stuck on what fifty minutes with a Year 10 class actually contains. This sets out one version: the question you open on, the assumptions you are trying to surface, what to do when a pupil says something explicit, and where a disclosure goes.


## What porn literacy means here


Porn literacy asks young people to look at pornography the way they would look at any other manufactured image — made by someone, for someone, to sell something. No pornography is shown in the classroom. The teaching happens entirely in the gap between what pupils have already seen and what they have taken from it without being asked.


That gap is wide. The Baroness Bertin Pornography Review, published in February 2025, found that over 23% of young people received no education on pornography in school at all. The Sex Education Forum's 2024 poll found that 58% of young people said they didn't learn enough about pornography in school, and 15% said pornography itself was their main source of information about sex. When school doesn't fill the gap, something else does.


## What the guidance asks for


The updated DfE statutory guidance, effective from September 2026, requires schools to teach about [pornography as part of their RSE curriculum](/topics/pornography-and-media-literacy), with the emphasis on how pornography can distort attitudes to sex, relationships and body image, and on building the ability to critically evaluate what's seen online.


The Bertin Review recommended that this education sits inside a broader curriculum covering healthy relationships, [consent](/blog/teaching-consent-beyond-no-means-no/), misogyny and media literacy. Take that seriously when you plan the year: one porn lesson with no consent teaching either side of it has very little to hold on to.


## The lesson


Open on where information comes from, not on pornography. Put one question to the class and take answers on the board: _where do people your age learn what sex is supposed to look like?_ You will get porn. You will also get friends, group chats, films, older siblings, music videos, and sometimes school. Write all of it up. Two things follow from that list without you having to argue for either — porn is one source among several rather than a special topic requiring a special lesson, and every pupil in the room has been able to answer without saying a word about themselves.


Then give them a method. The [Give & Get workshop I ran at Tate Modern](/blog/tate-modern-bedfellows-porn-literacy/) turns on five questions, and they transfer into a classroom unchanged: who made this, who is it aimed at, what is it selling, what is it leaving out, and what would it be like if you took it as instructions for real life. Run them first on something you can lawfully put on the screen — a music video, a perfume advert, a reality television edit — so the method is learned on material that is actually in the room. Then ask the class to run the same five questions on pornography, which stays out of the room. The fifth question is the one that does the work. Give it most of the time.


Three assumptions are worth surfacing by name rather than waiting for. The first is about bodies: that the bodies on screen are what bodies look like, and that a body which doesn't match is a body that's wrong. The second is about consent, which in pornography is settled before filming and invisible during it, so what a viewer learns to read as enthusiasm is a performance of enthusiasm and the negotiation that made it possible has been edited out. The third is the instruction problem — that the sequence on screen is what you are meant to do, in that order, and that wanting something different means something is wrong with you. The fifth question gets you all three in the pupils' own words, which is worth more than you naming them first.


Set the ground rule before any of this, and make it specific enough to point at later: nobody describes their own experience, and nobody asks anyone else about theirs. That gives you a rule to invoke rather than a judgement to make when someone crosses it.


### When a pupil says something explicit


Someone will. Sometimes it's a genuine answer and sometimes it's a bid for status in front of mates, and you often can't tell which in the moment, so respond the same way to both. Don't reprimand and don't look away from it. Take the content, restate it at the level of the question you asked, and hand it back to the class as analysis: a pupil who describes an act gets asked what that act assumes about the other person; a pupil who names a body part gets asked who that body is supposed to be for. Graphic detail stops being the currency of the room once it is treated as data.


What the class is reading while you do it is your face. Flinching tells them the topic is shameful and the adult can't cope, which is the opposite of what the lesson is for. If a comment was designed to shock, a flat and unbothered answer costs it its entire purpose.


### When a pupil discloses


This lesson invites disclosures that other lessons don't, so decide what you'll do before you're doing it. Three shapes come up. A pupil says they have seen something they can't stop thinking about. A pupil says they were shown something by someone older. A pupil says an image of them exists, or has been shared — which has [its own procedure](/blog/sharing-nudes-sextortion-deepfakes-schools-2026/) and shouldn't be improvised.


In the room the move is the same for all three: acknowledge it, don't take the details in front of the class, say plainly that you will help, and go to your designated safeguarding lead. Never promise a pupil you won't tell anyone ([paragraph 85](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=40)). The second of those three is a child protection concern rather than a curriculum one, and it doesn't wait for the end of the lesson.


Say the rule out loud at the start, alongside the ground rule: if something comes up that you have to pass on, you will tell the pupil you are passing it on. Nobody should find that out afterwards.


If you would rather not build the sequence from scratch, the BBFC and PSHE Association have free Key Stage 4 and 5 resources that give you a structured version of the same ground.


A first attempt at this will be a single well-planned session rather than a scheme of work, and that is the right size to start at.
