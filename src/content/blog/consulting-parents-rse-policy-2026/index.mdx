Since 1 September, consulting parents is a must rather than good practice. Schools "must proactively engage and consult parents when they develop and review their policy" ([paragraph 12 of the July 2025 RSHE guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5)).

The guidance then says almost nothing about what a consultation looks like. There is no vote, no threshold, no quorum, no minimum response rate and no prescribed method. That is worth knowing before you design one, because it means a local process is a local choice, and presenting one as a statutory requirement gives parents a wrong idea of where their contribution goes. If you want the wider picture of what changed, I wrote a [plain English summary of the guidance](/blog/2026-rse-guidance-plain-english-summary/).

## What consultation can and cannot change

"Parents are not able to veto curriculum content, but schools must consult with parents when developing their RSHE policy … and schools should respond positively to requests from parents to see material" ([paragraph 56](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)).

So relationships education, the statutory content and any safeguarding-led decision are not on the table. What is: the term a topic falls in, the notice families get before a sensitive lesson, the resources, who teaches it, the language used with younger children, and the support the school offers parents having the same conversation at home. In primary, the content of any sex education taught beyond the science curriculum is open as well.

I'd say which is which in the invitation itself. A parent who spends a consultation arguing about compulsory content has been given the wrong impression of what their answers can do.

## Explaining the teaching

Schools "should take steps to pro-actively engage parents and make sure they are aware of what is being taught in RSHE", by inviting them in to discuss the curriculum and any concerns, and supporting them in managing conversations at home ([paragraph 55](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=34)). Parents should also know about sex education content ahead of the lessons ([paragraph 15](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=6)).

A policy describes a subject. It does not show a parent what a lesson contains. The explanation that answers most questions is the specific one: year group, topic, what the lesson covers and what it does not, and where it sits in the sequence. A [puberty](/topics/puberty/) lesson in Year 5 is easier to discuss as a lesson than as a policy paragraph.

## Sharing the materials

Schools "should show parents a representative sample of the resources that they plan to use", and "should ensure that parents are able to view all curriculum materials used to teach RSHE on request" ([paragraph 56](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). A curriculum overview is not the sample, and the sample does not settle the on-request access.

A viewing session during the consultation, a parent portal login, or the scheme open on a laptop in reception all satisfy the first. The second needs a route that works when a request arrives out of the blue, and someone who knows it exists. If you have adapted a published scheme, show the version your classes will see and say which version it is.

Copyright governs how you share, with a portal or a presentation preferred over an open download, and it "should never be used as a reason to refuse to share" ([paragraphs 59 and 60](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). A provider clause that prevents you showing material to parents is void and unenforceable ([paragraphs 57 and 58](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=35)). Reading your contracts for those clauses has a lead time, and it sits on the wider [RSE policy checklist](/blog/rse-policy-checklist-2026/) with the rest of the review.

## Who the consultation reaches

A survey and an evening meeting reach different parents, and the returns from either will under-represent some families more than others. Parents who need a translation, parents of children with SEND, and families who do not attend school events can be missing from a set of responses that otherwise looks healthy.

Options worth considering: a translated summary, a question added to a meeting a family already attends, a conversation at collection time, or a phone call from someone the family knows. Record how many parents you reached and by which route, so the gaps are visible to whoever reads the consultation later.

The [letters, questionnaires and classroom events](/blog/reduce-withdrawals-sex-education-parent-consultation/) I use are a separate piece, including putting parents through an activity from the planned teaching.

## What to ask

A question like "do you support the RSE policy?" produces a number that cannot be acted on. Questions with somewhere to go are more useful to both sides:

- At what age do you think children should learn about this, and what sits behind that?
- What would you want to know before a lesson on this takes place?
- What would help you continue the conversation at home?
- Is there anything about your family's faith or culture we should understand in how we teach this?

Allow uncertainty and a choice not to answer, and offer a private route for anything about an individual child. Silence is not agreement, and should not be recorded as it.

## Responding afterwards

Write back with the themes parents raised, the changes you have made, and the things you are not changing with the reason attached. "This is statutory content" is a reason. So is a curriculum decision you can explain. Keep individual children out of the general response.

A set of near-identical responses is a set of views to record and weigh. It is not a measure of how many parents hold them, and not a reason to move something the guidance fixes. The same applies in reverse: a concern that arrives in an organised form can still be one a lot of parents share.

Withdrawal is a separate matter from consultation, and does not stand in for it. [How the right to withdraw works](/blog/parental-right-to-withdraw-rse-2026/) differs between primary and secondary, it does not cover relationships education or science, and a parent who takes part in your consultation keeps it either way.

## Primary has an addition

Primary schools "should consult parents about the content of anything that will be taught within sex education", along with supporting them to talk to their children about it and explaining the right to request withdrawal ([paragraph 31](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=12)). That is a consultation about specific lessons, not the policy in general. Sorting out which of your lessons count as sex education comes first, and that split is the opening job in [what the guidance changes for primary](/blog/2026-rse-guidance-primary-schools/).

## The record, and the next one

Keep a dated note of the methods used, how many parents responded, the themes, the changes made and the governors' approval of the final policy. The guidance does not ask for that record. It is a practical suggestion, and it is what a complaint or a governor's question is answered with later.

The duty covers developing and reviewing the policy, so this recurs. A fixed review date in the governance calendar is the difference between a consultation that happens in a difficult year and one that happens in every year. If you are briefing a board, [the questions governors ask](/blog/rse-policy-questions-governors-ask/) covers the ones that tend to come up.
