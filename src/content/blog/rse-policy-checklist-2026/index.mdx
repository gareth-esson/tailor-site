Review your RSE policy alongside this year's timetable and teaching materials. The task is to find where they agree, where they differ, and what needs updating against the current guidance.

This checklist is for the person doing that job: a PSHE lead, a deputy head, or a governor reading the policy properly for the first time. Each check names what the current guidance says, what to pull out of the file or the shared drive to test it, and what to do if the answer is missing.

The source throughout is the DfE's [Relationships Education, Relationships and Sex Education (RSE) and Health Education statutory guidance, July 2025](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf), which [has applied in England since 1 September 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education). Schools “must have regard to the guidance, and where they depart from those parts of the guidance which state that they should (or should not) do something they will need to have good reasons for doing so” (paragraph 5, printed page 3).

That distinction runs through everything below. A handful of these checks are duties the guidance states as a must. Most of paragraph 14, which is the closest thing to a contents list for an RSE policy, is written as a should: “The RSE policy should:” (printed page 4). Departing from a should is allowed with good reasons, and those reasons are worth writing down where the policy departs.

A few checks are neither. They are practical suggestions for making the evidence easy to find, and they say so.

## How to use it

Work down the list with the policy open and one recent term's planning beside it. Answer each check with a document, not a recollection. Where a check fails, the action line is the next step, not a project.

Finishing the list records what you found against the passages named here, including where the policy, the plan and the teaching need attention. It is not legal certification, a compliance guarantee, or a substitute for your own legal advice where a specific situation needs it.

## The policy the guidance requires

### 1. An up-to-date written policy exists

*Applies to: all schools in England, which the guidance defines as “all schools, whether maintained, non-maintained or independent schools, including academies and free schools, non-maintained special schools, maintained special schools and alternative provision, including pupil referral units” (paragraph 7, printed page 3). The statutory requirements “do not apply to sixth form colleges, 16-19 academies or Further Education (FE) colleges”, though FE and sixth form colleges providing full-time education for 14 to 16 year olds do need to follow this guidance (paragraph 11 and footnote 6, printed page 4). A maintained school with its own sixth form is inside the scope: paragraph 9 lists “governing bodies of maintained schools (including schools with a sixth form)” among the bodies this guidance is aimed at (printed page 3).*

“All schools must have an up-to-date written policy for relationships education or, where they teach sex education, for RSE” ([paragraph 12, printed page 4](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=5)). Schools “are not required to publish a policy for health education”, although “it would be good practice for schools to set out some of the details about how health education will be taught” (paragraph 13, printed page 4).

**Evidence:** the policy document itself, with the date it was approved and the date it is next due. Check that the file staff work from is the version approved through the process named in the policy.

**If it's a gap:** identify the current version, the approver and the review process named in your policy, then arrange the missing review.

### 2. It is published, and free to anyone who asks

*Applies to: all schools. The immediate exception is for schools without a website.*

Schools “must provide a copy of their policy free of charge to anyone who asks for one and publish the policy on the school website” (paragraph 12, printed page 4). The footnote adds: “If a school does not have a website, they should ensure that the policy is available by other means” (footnote 7, printed page 4).

**Evidence:** find the policy on the public site in a browser you are not logged into, and check the office knows where to send a parent who telephones. Compare the published PDF's date against the approved version from check 1.

**If it's a gap:** replace the published file with the current version. If there is no school website, record in the policy which other means you use.

### 3. Parents were consulted, and will be again

*Applies to: all schools. Primary schools teaching sex education have an additional consultation expectation.*

“Schools must proactively engage and consult parents when they develop and review their policy, ensuring parents understand that effective RSHE is important for promoting and protecting the wellbeing of all children” (paragraph 12, printed page 4). Schools “should take steps to pro-actively engage parents and make sure they are aware of what is being taught in RSHE” (paragraph 55, printed page 33). Primary schools “should consult parents about the content of anything that will be taught within sex education”, including support for parents in talking to their children and advice about the right to request withdrawal (paragraph 31, printed page 11).

Consultation is not a vote. “Parents are not able to veto curriculum content, but schools must consult with parents when developing their RSHE policy” (paragraph 56, printed page 34).

**Evidence:** a dated record of the last consultation, the questions parents raised, and what changed in the policy as a result.

**If it's a gap:** the guidance sets no format, quorum or notice period, so schedule the consultation alongside the next review and keep the record from the start. A dated note of questions and responses is a suggestion for making this checkable later, not a requirement of the guidance.

## What paragraph 14 says the policy should contain

### 4. Content, timing, staffing and external providers

*Applies to: all schools. Paragraph 14 is a should throughout.*

The policy should “Set out the subject content, how and when it will be taught, and who is responsible for teaching it, including any external providers the school will use” (paragraph 14, first bullet, printed page 4).

**Evidence:** the section of the policy that names topics and year groups, next to this year's timetable and the list of visitors booked for the rest of the year.

**If it's a gap:** name the year groups, the terms and the lead for each phase. A policy that describes the subject without saying when it happens cannot be checked against practice.

### 5. The relationships and sex education split, and the right to withdraw

*Applies to: all schools, though primary sex education is optional. “Sex education is not compulsory in primary schools, but we recommend that primaries teach sex education in years 5 and/or 6” (paragraph 30, printed page 11).*

The policy should “Differentiate between relationships and sex education (where sex education is taught), so that parents have clear information. Relationships education doesn't involve explaining the detail of different forms of sexual activity, but can cover sensitive topics such as sexual violence in order to keep children safe.” It should also “Include information about a parent's right to request that their child is withdrawn from sex education” (paragraph 14, second and third bullets, printed page 4).

Withdrawal concerns sex education, including optional primary provision; check 10 sets out the primary and secondary arrangements. “Parents do not have the right to withdraw their pupils from relationships and health education, nor can they be withdrawn from topics taught as part of the science curriculum, including science topics related to puberty or sexual reproduction” (paragraph 21, printed page 6).

**Evidence:** a unit-by-unit list showing which strand each lesson sits in, and the paragraph in the policy a parent would read before making a request.

**If it's a gap:** label the strands before a request arrives rather than during one.

### 6. Accessibility, and questions the curriculum doesn't answer

*Applies to: all schools, mainstream and special. The SEND expectation “applies to both mainstream and special schools” (paragraph 75, printed page 37).*

The policy should “Explain how content will be made accessible to all pupils, including those with special educational needs or disabilities (SEND)”, and should “Explain how teachers will answer questions about topics in sex education that the school does not cover (in primary) or that relates to sex education from which the child has been withdrawn” (paragraph 14, fourth and seventh bullets, printed pages 4 and 5). On the second of those, paragraph 87 adds that the policy should explain how teachers handle such questions “with an emphasis on supporting the child”, which “may include asking a pupil to speak to their parents or a trusted adult, signposting to support services where needed” (printed page 40).

**Evidence:** the accessibility wording in the policy, tested against one actual adapted unit. Ask a teacher what they say to a Year 6 question the scheme doesn't cover, and see whether their answer matches the policy.

**If it's a gap:** write the answer the school wants teachers to give, and cover it in the next subject meeting.

### 7. Monitoring, materials access, approval and review

*Applies to: all schools.*

Paragraph 14 says the policy should: “Describe how the subject is monitored and evaluated”, “Set out how parents can view curriculum materials”, and “Explain how the policy has been produced, who approves the policy, and how and when it will be reviewed” (paragraph 14, fifth, sixth and eighth bullets, printed page 5).

**Evidence:** the named monitoring method and who does it, the route a parent uses to see materials, and the minute or other record of approval.

**If it's a gap:** name a monitoring method the school already uses, such as a book look or a planning scrutiny, rather than inventing a new system for the policy's sake.

## The practice behind the document

### 8. Materials a parent could see this week

*Applies to: all schools. Copyright conditions attach to how materials are shared, not to whether they are shared.*

Schools “should show parents a representative sample of the resources that they plan to use” and “should ensure that parents are able to view all curriculum materials used to teach RSHE on request” (paragraph 56, printed page 34). “When contracting with external providers, schools should not agree to any contractual restrictions on showing parents any content that the school will use” (paragraph 57, printed page 34). Where copyright applies, schools must comply with it, with a portal or presentation named as best practice and take-home copies as the alternative, and “copyright law affects the way in which materials are shared, but should never be used as a reason to refuse to share them” (paragraphs 59 and 60, printed page 34).

**Evidence:** what a parent asking today would be shown, and the contract or licence terms for every external resource in this year's plan.

**If it's a gap:** check the sharing route against the conditions in paragraphs 59 and 60 before promising parents access. Raise any restrictive clause with the provider.

### 9. External providers checked before they arrive

*Applies to: all schools using visitors or bought-in materials. The safeguarding agreement is a must.*

“Schools are responsible for checking the credentials of any visitor or visiting organisation. Schools should ask to see materials and a lesson plan in advance, and should seek the views of parents, making sure that all materials can be viewed by parents” (paragraph 52, printed page 33). Working with external organisations can help, “but schools always remain responsible for the content and the way in which children are taught” (paragraph 52). And: “Where lessons are delivered by external agencies, schools must agree in advance of the session how a safeguarding concern would be dealt with by the external visitor” (paragraph 84, printed page 39).

**Evidence:** for each visitor booked this year, who checked the credentials, the lesson plan you were sent, and the written agreement on how a disclosure in that session is handled.

**If it's a gap:** settle the safeguarding agreement before the next visit, not after it.

### 10. Withdrawal handled as the guidance describes

*Applies to: primary and secondary differently, and the decision sits with the head teacher.*

“Where primary schools provide sex education, head teachers must automatically grant a request to withdraw a pupil from it, other than content that is taught as part of the science curriculum. In secondary, head teachers can refuse a request in exceptional circumstances, for example because of safeguarding concerns or a pupil's specific vulnerability” (paragraph 18, printed page 6). “From three terms before the pupil turns 16, a pupil can choose to opt back into sex education even if their parent has requested withdrawal”, and “Schools should ensure that pupils know they have this option” (paragraph 19, printed page 6). Where a pupil is withdrawn, “it is the school's responsibility to ensure that the pupil receives appropriate, purposeful education during the period of withdrawal” (paragraph 20, printed page 6).

**Evidence:** the record of any requests, the discussion the guidance says schools “will want to document” (paragraph 17, printed page 6), and what withdrawn pupils actually did during those lessons.

**If it's a gap:** name the alternative work and the supervising member of staff in advance of the unit.

### 11. Every statutory topic covered, and sequenced early enough

*Applies to: all schools for relationships education and RSE. Health education is compulsory “in all schools except independent schools”, where “Personal, social, health and economic education (PSHE) continues to be compulsory” instead, and the health education guidance applies to academies and free schools (paragraph 2, printed page 2; footnotes 4 and 5, printed page 3).*

“Schools should cover all statutory topics, recognising that young people can start developing healthy behaviour and relationship skills as soon as they start school. Schools should sequence teaching so that pupils are supported and equipped with the knowledge to navigate different experiences in a positive way before they occur, and to prevent harms” (paragraph 15d, printed page 5). The lead teacher “will need to work closely with colleagues in related curriculum areas to ensure the subjects complement and do not duplicate content covered in national curriculum subjects such as citizenship, science, computing and PE” (paragraph 51, printed page 33).

**Evidence:** a coverage map running the guidance's content tables against your scheme of work, showing where each item is taught and by whom.

**If it's a gap:** the map is the work. Build it once, in the format the school already uses for curriculum plans, and the rest of this list becomes easier to answer.

### 12. Teaching that meets the equality duties

*Applies to: all schools for the non-discrimination requirement. “Independent schools that are not academies are not subject to the PSED” (footnote 13, printed page 36).*

“Schools are required to comply with relevant requirements of the Equality Act 2010, including the Public sector equality duty (PSED) (s.149), when teaching RSHE”, and “Schools must ensure topics in RSHE are taught in a way which does not discriminate against pupils or amount to harassment” (paragraphs 64 and 65, printed page 36).

**Evidence:** the RSHE content that covers protected characteristics, and how teachers are briefed on paragraphs 69 to 72. Pupils “should also be taught the facts and the law about biological sex and gender reassignment”, including that people with the protected characteristic of gender reassignment “have protection from discrimination and should be treated with respect and dignity” (paragraph 69). Beyond those facts and the law “there is significant debate”, and schools “should be careful not to endorse any particular view or teach it as fact” (paragraph 70). Read the full passages, including the expectations for respectful discussion and external resources in paragraphs 71 and 72 (printed pages 36–37).

**If it's a gap:** treat it as a briefing question for the people teaching it, and check the school's equality information mentions RSHE.

### 13. Some evidence of what pupils have learnt

*Applies to: all schools.*

The guidance folds assessment into curriculum design rather than giving it a section: effective teaching breaks knowledge and skills “into manageable and well-sequenced units, including opportunities for pupils to practise skills”, builds “sequentially, with regular feedback provided on pupil progress”, and “assessments should identify where pupils need extra support or intervention” (paragraph 50, printed pages 32–33).

**Evidence:** whatever the school already uses to know what pupils took away from a unit, whether that is end-of-unit work, a baseline and revisit, or a moderated book look.

**If it's a gap:** pick one unit this term and start there. Paragraph 50 does not prescribe a separate RSHE assessment system.

### 14. Staff who can teach it, and are supported when it gets difficult

*Applies to: all schools, including where teaching is bought in.*

The curriculum “should be delivered by school staff or, where schools choose to use them, external providers who have the knowledge, skills and confidence to create a safe and supportive environment and to facilitate participative and interactive education which aims to support and not to alarm pupils. Staff should be trained in safeguarding and offering support, recognising the increased possibility of disclosures” (paragraph 15f, printed page 5). Paragraph 87 adds that “Teachers may require support and training in handling questions that are better not dealt with in the classroom” (printed page 40). Discussions about sensitive topics “can lead to increased safeguarding reports”, and all staff should know what to do if they have concerns (paragraph 84, printed page 39).

**Evidence:** the list of staff teaching RSHE this year against the training they have had, and what a teacher does with a disclosure that arrives ten minutes into a lesson.

**If it's a gap:** start with the staff teaching the most sensitive units this term.

## When the list is finished

A completed checklist is a record of what you found on a particular date, against the passages named above. It shows where the policy, the plan and the teaching agree, and where they need attention. It is not legal certification and does not establish compliance on its own.

If the coverage map or the policy rewrite is the part with no obvious owner, [talk to us about your school's RSE policy and curriculum planning](/services/rse-policy-curriculum-planning/).
