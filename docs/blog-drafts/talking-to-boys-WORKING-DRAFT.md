Most boys are doing fine. They're not radicalised, and they don't hate women. They still have crushes, still love their mums, and are still working out who they are. A lot of the conversation around masculinity in schools starts from the assumption that something has gone badly wrong with boys; I don't believe it has. I think young people have a lot to contend with, but most are still kids who need support.


If you are here for the classroom practice rather than the argument, it starts at [Teaching it well](#teaching-it-well).


What has changed is what boys are swimming in online. A boy does not have to seek out harmful content for the algorithm to deliver it straight to his feed. Researchers led by UCL created TikTok accounts posing as teenage boys potentially vulnerable to radicalisation, with interests such as masculinity or loneliness, and watched the For You page over five days. The share of recommended videos with misogynistic content rose roughly fourfold ([UCL, February 2024](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2024/feb/social-media-algorithms-amplify-misogynistic-content-teens)). That's not an account of what real boys watch, but it shows how quickly the feed can move towards this material without a boy looking for it.


The content competing for boys' attention is not neutral. It's shaped by what performs well, not what's best for them. Many of the loudest voices telling boys how to be a man are monetised, and nuance is harder to sell. Certainty travels better: simple answers, clear enemies, a sense that something has been hidden from you. The men who say _it depends_ are competing with men like Andrew Tate, whose appeal rests partly on a much more seductive proposition: _I know the truth, and everyone else is lying to you_.


A thirteen-year-old is ill-equipped to see through that, and not because he is gullible. He has less experience judging credibility, less practice spotting manipulation, and far less context for understanding how displays of wealth, status and online influence are manufactured. The evidence appears to be right there in front of him: the body, the car, the watch, the women, the holidays and a follower count in the millions. All of it can be presented as proof that this man knows something worth knowing and that his way of living works.


But very little of that proof can be independently checked. A boy cannot know which parts are real, whether the money came from following the method or from selling the method, or how many people followed the same advice and got nowhere. Those men are missing from the picture.


Boys have been asked directly what they think of Andrew Tate, and his reach is much greater than his approval. Of British boys aged 13 to 15, 84% had heard of him. Of the same group, 23% had a good view and 53% a bad one ([YouGov Children's Omnibus, September 2023](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/47419-one-in-six-boys-aged-6-15-have-a-positive-view-of-andrew-tate)). The subgroup was small, 158 boys, so those percentages are estimates rather than precise measures of all boys that age.


Recognition is not endorsement. More than twice as many boys that age had a bad view of Tate as a good one. But 23% is still a substantial minority. To put that finding in context, I gathered [sixteen findings from five surveys](/blog/what-do-boys-actually-think/) looking at what boys think and experience around masculinity, misogyny and the manosphere. The evidence does not describe a generation of boys in crisis. Nor does it describe a problem schools can afford to ignore. It shows why the work has to be done, and done well.


The statutory RSHE guidance applying from September 2026 puts this in the secondary curriculum. Pupils should be equipped to recognise [misogyny](/topics/masculinity-and-misogyny/), and should have an opportunity to discuss the possible influence of particular sub-cultures on sexual ethics, with incels and online influencers named ([guidance page 14](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=15)).


## What doesn't work


Lecturing boys about toxic masculinity. In my experience the term tends to make some of them defensive before you've said anything useful. If a boy hears "toxic masculinity" and thinks you're calling _him_ toxic, you've lost him. The guidance puts it better: where misogynistic ideas are expressed at school, staff should "challenge the ideas, rather than the person expressing them" ([paragraph 80](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=39)).


Hanging the whole topic on a one-off assembly or a drop-down day. Pupils argue their way towards a position on this rather than receiving one, and a single event leaves the disagreement it provokes with nowhere to go. Plan what follows it: when pupils can return to the questions, who will pick those up, and where they can get help. If a visiting specialist starts the conversation, agree how the school will continue it afterwards.


Framing the conversation as "boys need to be better." Boys who are already decent switch off because they don't recognise themselves in it. Boys who are genuinely struggling feel attacked. Neither group gets what they need, and the guidance asks teachers to avoid language stigmatising boys or suggesting boys and men are always the perpetrators ([paragraph 79](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=39)). The same goes for the girls in the room: nobody should be asked to argue for why they deserve respect.


## Teaching it well


Giving boys space to talk honestly, over time, with someone they trust. Build in chances to revisit an idea, ask a question privately or use an anonymous question box. Keep familiar staff involved so a conversation has somewhere to continue after the planned lesson.


Being careful what you call the problem. In one session, a boy said to me, "But what if I want to be hench and sleep with lots of women? Is that bad? Am I wrong?" My instinct was to correct or reframe, and that instinct is the one to watch. Wanting to be strong, and wanting to be wanted, are ordinary things to want at thirteen. A boy who is told those wants are the problem hears that he is the problem.


What does the damage is the version of manhood built around them. It hands those wants over as requirements rather than choices. A boy who does not want them has to act as though he does. A boy who does want them is expected to put them ahead of everything else, including how he treats people and how much help he is allowed to ask for. That is what a lesson can take apart, and it can be done without telling a boy that what he wants is wrong.


Starting from what boys actually care about rather than what we think they should care about. Friendships, status, fitting in, feeling confident, being respected. These are the entry points. A thirteen-year-old rarely arrives at this material through its theory of gender. He arrives through a promise: confidence, sex, money, respect, certainty, somewhere to belong. Misogyny and gender norms become relevant when you connect them to things boys already think about, not when you present them as abstract social problems.


Using prepared examples to examine the ideas. The guidance asks teachers to avoid pointing pupils directly to specific content or its creators ([paragraph 81](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf#page=39)). A clearly fictional message can give pupils an argument to evaluate without sending them towards the person promoting it. Choose the example for the technique you want to examine (certainty, grievance, humiliation, status, invented statistics, us and them) and prepare the debrief. Allow for pupils who are encountering the idea for the first time.


Giving them something to be, not only something to avoid. The same paragraph expects opportunities to develop positive conceptions of masculinity and femininity, including learning from positive male role models, and says plainly that most boys and young men are respectful to girls and each other. Boys hear plenty about the sort of man not to be, and much less about the sort they could be.


Setting homework that makes them try it. Call a male friend just to see how he is. Next time you are down, ask a parent to talk. Notice a boy having a bad day and ask him about it. Go back to something you dropped because of how it would look. In a one-off session this is close to rhetorical, and it still does something, because naming the task shows a room what the alternative looks like in practice. In a sequence where you see them the following week, you can ask how it went, and that is when it stops being rhetorical.


Boys need enough room to look at the versions of masculinity they are offered, turn down the ones that cost them or somebody else, and put something better in their place. That is a larger job than convincing them misogyny is wrong, and it is the one that lasts.
